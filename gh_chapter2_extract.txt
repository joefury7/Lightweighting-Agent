3.1 Introduction and Summary
In Chapter 9 of Volume 1, we consider a variety of ways in which to mount mirrors in 
the size range up to about 20 in. (51 cm). A few of those techniques are applicable, within 
limits, to larger elements but totally inadequate for really large mirrors. The important 
criteria for mounting suitability are allowable deflection due to gravity, thermal effects, 
and performance level required. When the aperture is modest, the thickness and material 
choice conducive to high stiffness, and the performance requirements low, the optic can be 
considered a rigid body and mounted semikinematically or with a multiple point mount, 
such as the Hindle mount. Larger mirrors are more flexible and so require more careful 
consideration. In any practical application, large mirrors are susceptible to deformations 
due to temperature gradients, thermal property inhomogeneities, internal stresses, and 
imposed forces such as acceleration and gravity. Changes in orientation of the optic dur -
ing use within the Earth’s gravitational field are also of significance, as are variations in 
thermal loading. Release of gravitational effects must be considered in the case of mir -
rors fabricated, tested, and mounted on Earth and then launched into space. The largest 
gravitationally caused deformations of the mounted mirror occur when gravity acts along 
the optic axis. Back supports are used in astronomical and similar instruments that point 
upward or downward from the horizontal to counter these effects. That situation is the 
subject of Chapter 4. The more general case of mounting mirrors for variable axis orienta-
tion is discussed in Chapter 5. Design details for metallic mirrors and the unique aspects 
of mountings for those mirrors are discussed separately in Chapter 6.
The fixed, horizontal-axis mirror is also deformed by gravity. The deformations that 
occur in this case are not rotationally symmetrical about the mirror axis. In this chapter, we

If the cross section of the mirror is not of uniform thickness, that is, concave (as indicated 
in Figure 3.1b) or convex, the transmitted forces also produce moments that tend to bend 
the mirror. These are illustrated schematically in view (c), which shows an element of aver-

producing an elemental moment. When integrated over the entire mirror, these elements 
have a resultant force equal to Wξ, where ξ is the distance from the center of mass to the 
midplane (dashed vertical line in Figure 3.1b). This resultant is balanced by a distribution 
of bending moments mR, at the mirror edge as indicated at the bottom of view (b). These 
moments form the second type of boundary forces due to gravity. They tend to bend the 
mirror surface.
Schwesinger also gave a theory for calculating the surface deformations and the result -
ing reflected wavefront errors caused by these two types of forces. Shear stresses and the 
effects of central perforations in the mirrors were not considered. Subject to these limita -
tions, we will apply that theory to explain the advantages and disadvantages of a variety 
of mechanical mounts for horizontal-axis mirrors of various configurations and sizes.
An alternative method for determining self-weight deformation of mirrors in the axis-
horizontal position was developed by Nelson (1970), based on elastic theory due to Bleich 
(1968). This method is for mirrors with a parabolic optical surface and can be used to 
determine the effect of a central hole. Shear effects are also included in this method. Even 
for thick mirrors with aspect ratios of between 7 and 10, the additional deformation from 
shear is small, about 4%, and can be neglected for most first-order analysis. A comparison 
between Schwesinger’s and Nelson’s methods shows agreement to about 20%. In this chap-
ter, Schwesinger’s method is used, since it is the most commonly used analytical method 
and is also the simplest.

horizontal, cylindrical posts located symmetrically with respect to the vertical centerline. 
In the mount shown in Figure 3.2, a third post is provided at top center of the mirror. It 
normally does not contact the mirror but supports a clip extending in front of the mirror’s 
rim to prevent the mirror from falling forward if bumped. It is advantageous to use a 
sleeve of plastic material such as Kevlar as the contacting surface on the posts to provide 
a slightly resilient interface and some thermal insulation and to reduce friction. Rollers 
are also used sometimes to minimize friction. The contacts between the posts and the 
mirror rim are between different diameter parallel-axis cylinders if the mirror is circular. 
This type of mount is called a V-mount since it functions as if the circular mirror rim sits 
in a V-block. A rectangular mirror can also be supported in such a mount. The contacts 
at the posts are then between cylinders and a flat surface. In each case, the axial position 
of the mirror is maintained by clamping its rim lightly with clips on each of the lower 
posts. Each clip and the corresponding pad immediately behind it on the back plate of the 
mount contact only small areas on the mirror so as to minimize local bending moments 
should the interfacing surfaces not be exactly parallel. Springs may be used to provide 
the clamping forces.

3.5 Ideal Radial Mount
An ideal mount for horizontal-axis circular mirrors of larger size was defined by 
Schwesinger (1954) as one in which the disk is balanced by radially directed forces 
around its periphery. The magnitudes of these forces vary as the cosine of the polar 
angle φ measured from the downward-pointing radius of the disk. The radial forces are 
maximum compressive at the bottom of the vertical centerline, decrease to zero at both 
sides on the horizontal centerline, and then change sign to tensile forces of increasing 
magnitude, reaching a common maximum at the top of the disk. Figure 3.12 illustrates 
this condition for a large 4 m (157 in.) diameter perforated mirror analyzed by Malvick 
and Pearson (1968). The contour lines represent mirror surface displacements and indi -
cate that the surface becomes astigmatic owing to edge moments generated by gravity. 
Similar deformations, of lesser magnitude of course, occur in smaller solid mirrors when 
similarly mounted.
The analytical method used by Malvick and Pearson (1968) included shear effects as 
well as those of a large, central hole in a solid disk mirror. Using an analytical method 
called dynamic relaxation (DR) as developed by Day (1965), Otter et al. (1996), and Malvick 
(1968) and a tensor formulation, Malvick and Pearson expressed the 3D equations of elas-
ticity as three equilibrium equations and six stress displacement  equations. The body of 
the mirror was divided into a reasonable number of nonorthogonal curvilinear elements.

3.6 Mercury Tube Mounts
An approximation to the ideal mount is to support the mirror’s edge by radially directed 
compressive forces of variable magnitude proportional to (1 + cos φ), where φ is the polar 
angle measured from the downward-pointing radius. Figure 3.14 illustrates the forces 
applied and the resultant surface contour deformation for the same 4 m (157 in.) diameter 
mirror that was the basis for the analysis of the ideal mount depicted in Figure 3.12. This 
type of force field results approximately when the mirror is floated within an annular mer-
cury-filled tube located between the mirror rim and a rigid cylindrical cell wall. The width 
of the tube is chosen so that when it is nearly full of mercury, the mirror will be floated. 
Typical designs call for flattened, neoprene-coated, Dacron tubes to hold the mercury. The 
axial location of the tube center should coincide with the plane through the center of grav-
ity of the mirror so that overturning moments are avoided.

centered within 0.0005 in. (0.012 mm) in astronomical telescopes with mercury tube radial

rollers or an insulating elastic layer between the rollers and the mirror edge. Plastic 
rollers will take a permanent deformation or set with the passage of time, increasing 
friction. Friction is also increased with the use of an elastic layer between the mirror 
edge and roller chain. Conventional roller chain, sold as conveyor chain with oversize 
rollers, employing steel rollers, is preferred.

chain. A wide variety of chain sizes and load capacities are available, and are relatively 
low in cost. Special chain links are available to permit the attachment of spacers and 
safeties to the roller chain. Roller chain supports are very compact, taking space around 
the mirror edge equal to the chain thickness. For optical shop testing, a roller chain 
permits ease of rotation of the mirror in its support to test for astigmatism.

sible local fracture, is a drawback of the roller chain support. Careful installation and 
adjustment of the roller chain minimizes potential fracture at the mirror edge.

strength of 3700 lbs, is suitable for a 56 in. [1.42 m] diameter solid fused silica mirror 9 in. 
[22.9 cm] thick, assuming a safety factor of four.

with respect to the mirror, and connects the support to the rest of the mirror mount. 
The chain hanger provides three adjustments: location of the centroid of the two chains

The previously mentioned DR analytical method used by Malvick and Pearson (1968) to 
determine the surface deformations of large mirrors due to terrestrial gravity effects was 
for many years the only available method to evaluate proposed or actual mirror and mount 
designs. The results of their analyses using this method have proved extremely useful to

opto-mechanical designers and engineers engaged in evaluations of smaller or larger mir-

4.1 Introduction and Summary
Mirror mounts have two chief functions: maintaining the correct position of the optical 
element with respect to the rest of the optical system and controlling deformation of the 
optical surface from external forces to an acceptable level. For small mirrors, these two 
functions are combined in a single mount, usually using kinematic principles, such as 
those discussed in Chapter 10 of Volume 1. For large mirrors, different mechanisms are 
used for the two functions. This chapter addresses mirror mounts intended to minimize 
the effect of self-weight deflection from gravity forces acting normal to the mirror surface, 
that is, when the mirror is in a vertical-axis position.
What is discussed here applies to both small and large mirrors. Included are designs 
for single- and multiple-ring mounts with continuous and discrete point contacts with the 
mirror, three-point supports, Hindle mounts, counterweighted mounts, pneumatic and 
hydraulic (piston, bladder, and compliant tube) mounts, as well as metrology mounts.

4.2 Conceptual Design
The principles of design for a mirror mount are discussed in Chapter 1. The first important 
part of this process is conceptual, and second is first-order analysis. For a mirror mount in 
a vertical-axis position, optical surface deflection due to gravity (called self-weight deflec-
tion) is the most important parameter. Conceptual or rough order of magnitude analysis and 
first-order analysis are performed using plate bending theory. The accuracy of this theory 
in the context of mirror self-weight deflection is limited by several factors. Classical plate 
bending theory assumes that the plate is thin, typically with a diameter-to-thickness ratio 
of 10 or more and is in the form of a plane parallel plate. Real mirrors typically have signifi-
cant optical surface curvature, vary in thickness over the aperture, and may have discon -
tinuity features such as central holes. Choice of material determines Poisson’s ratio, which 
somewhat affects deflection sensitivity. Even so, this theory is adequate for early design.
Additional sources of error, such as lightweight structures and contoured backs, are dis-
cussed in Chapter 2, Lightweight Mirror Configurations. Deflection of nonaxisymmetric 
mirrors can be analyzed using plate bending theory that has been modified for the limita-
tions discussed here.
Variation in mirror thickness usually does not introduce significant error for most 
 conceptual and first-order analysis, as shown by a study made by Wan et al. (1989). A plane 
parallel  axisymmetric 1.8 m diameter lightweight borosilicate mirror deflected 768 nm when 
on an optimized three-point support. In comparison, the thickness variation of 31% over an f/1 
optical surface changed the deflection of the mirror to 694 nm, that is, a change of about 10%. 
Typically, the change in mirror thickness is not as great as in this example. Hence, for concep-
tual and even for most first-order analysis, the effect of thickness variation can be ignored.
Self-weight deflection is changed by the presence of a central hole. This is discussed in 
the context of ring and multipoint mounts. Considering deflection just from bending for 
common mirror materials such as Corning 7740 Pyrex and Schott Zerodur, a central hole 
measuring up to about 50% of the mirror diameter changes the deflection about 10% when 
compared to the same mirror without a central hole. Deflection from a central hole can 
be ignored for conceptual analysis, while a correction is desirable for first-order analysis.
A change in Poisson’s ratio also changes the deflection of a mirror with a central hole. 
When supported on a continuous ring around its circumference, the deflection of a mirror 
with a central hole that is 30% of the mirror diameter is about 90% of that for a solid mirror 
if the mirror material Poisson ratio is 0.17 (typical of fused silica). If the mirror is made of 
aluminum, has the same size hole, and has a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3, the deflection is reduced 
to about 84%. This indicates that the effect of changes in Poisson’s ratio is small. From fused 
silica, with a Poisson’s ratio of 0.17 , to aluminum, with a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3, the change in 
bending is about 6% for most mounting configurations. Therefore, changes in Poisson’s ratio 
can be neglected in conceptual design. Corrections are desirable for first-order analysis.
4.3 Ring Mounts
Continuous-ring mounts and closely spaced discrete point approximations thereof are 
acceptable supports for fixed, vertical-axis mirrors if the deflections of unsupported 
regions of the surface due to gravity are within tolerance for the specific application.

Another necessary condition for acceptability is that the resulting radius (i.e., optical 
power) change can be tolerated or compensated for by refocusing. Nonsymmetrical mir -
rors, such as those with elliptical or rectangular apertures, do not possess this symmetry 
and sag more along the longer dimension, thereby becoming astigmatic as well as chang -
ing in power.
The simplest type of ring support is obtained when a solid mirror is supported on 
its back by a continuous line contact near the mirror’s edge, as illustrated in Figure 4.1. 
If the mirror is very stiff and the mount surface contacting the mirror is not as true as 
the glass surface (undoubtedly the case), the actual contact will be at the three highest 
points along the line. The mirror would then bridge over those points. Generally, it is 
assumed that the mount is stiffer than the mirror and that it does not deform because 
of the mirror’s weight. In real life, all mirrors and most mounts are somewhat flexible, 
so they do deform, and contact may occur more or less continuously along the ring. 
Because both the mirror and the mount are elastic, the contact is not a line but actu -
ally a narrow localized zone, as discussed in our considerations of contact stress in 
Chapter 11 of Volume 1.
A compliant ring provides even distribution of support all around its diameter. One 
type of a compliant ring used in mounting large mirrors is a tube full of liquid or inflated 
with high pressure gas. For example, an 80 in. diameter Pyrex mirror with an aspect ratio 
of 8 supported on a continuous ring at the 68% diameter (0.68 × 80 in. = 54.4 in.) could be 
supported by a 2 in. diameter flexible hose working at 50 psi. This assumes that the hose 
deforms to about 0.6 of its diameter, which is typical. Flexible tube mounts are used for 
axial support of some large telescope mirrors such as the 4 m Mayall at Kitt Peak National 
Observatory in Arizona.
A drawback of the flexible tube mounting, which is shared by other isostatic mounts, is a 
lack of stability in position. It is necessary, as mentioned elsewhere in this book, to provide 
hard points to define the location of the mirrors. These hard points normally are designed 
using kinematic principles. Load cells are often incorporated in those point supports to 
monitor forces exerted on the mirror in order to insure that the defining points do not 
induce unacceptable surface deformations.

4.4 Air Bag (Bladder) Mounts
Pneumatic (air bag or bladder) supports have been used in many astronomical telescope 
primary mirror mounts as a means of distributing axial forces over the back surface of the 
mirror. They are of three general types: those with large area contact provided by flat, air-
filled bladders; those with annular area contacts at two or more selected radial zones; and 
those with multiple, pie-shaped segments. Each of these types is considered here.
Single bladder supports typically are made of two sheets of nonporous material such 
as neoprene or neoprene-coated fabric such as Dacron, joined and sealed together at the 
edges as shown in Figure 4.7 . Vukobratovich (1992) indicated that a special variety of 
ozone-resistant neoprene is needed if the air bag is to be used at a high altitude, such as in 
mountaintop observatories.
The mirror’s axial location and orientation are usually referenced to three, small-area, 
pad-type, adjustable supports (called hard or defining points) projecting from the cell back 
structure through sealed holes (not shown) in the bladder. The bladder supports 90%–95% 
of the mirror’s weight, and the remaining weight is supported by the hard points. A low-
pressure pump supplies air to the bladder through a pressure regulator. In some cases, a 
spare pump that is automatically switched into operation if the main pump fails provides 
redundancy. The pumps are operated considerably below maximum rated pressure and at 
lower than full speed. This increases useful life.

4.5.1 Three-Point Mounts
The simplest mount for a vertical-axis mirror of symmetrical configuration provides sup -
port at three localized points on the mirror’s back as sketched in Figure 4.15. If symmetry 
is to be preserved, the three points should lie on a circle centered about the mirror’s axis 
and at equal (120°) angular intervals around the circle.
The deformation distribution on the mirror surface due to gravity differs in mag -
nitude and configuration with the radius of the support circle. Figure 4.16a shows the 
typical warped surface obtained from a three-point support with those points at the 
96% zone of a 4 m (158 in.) diameter solid disk mirror with a 1.32 m (52 in.) diam -
eter central hole. The diameter-to-thickness ratio was approximately 7:1. Malvick and 
Pearson (1968) stated that the example was intended to illustrate the effect of using 
three fixed, that is, hard, points to define the location of a mirror otherwise uniformly 
supported. If these points are used to support a small fraction (say 1%) of the weight 
of this mirror, the p–v deflection of the surface would be determined approximately 
from the contour line count and scale factor as [14 − (−8)(10 −4 cm)(10−2)] = 0.22 µm = 0.41 
wave for green light.
Figure 4.16b shows the deflections produced by mounting the same mirror on three hard 
points located at the 72.5% zone. The p–v deflection range is now only [4 − (−2)(10 −4 cm)
(10−2)] = 0.06 µm = 0.11 wave. If mounted near the center hole (see Figure 4.16c), the deflec-
tion of the mirror edge would be 0.22  µm, that is, the same as the center deflection with 
support at the edge. In all these cases, the location of the zero contour was arbitrary. The 
deflection contour patterns of a mirror lightly clamped to the support points would appear 
the same as for the unclamped mirror if the clamping forces are normal to the contacted 
surfaces and pass directly through the centers of the contacting areas. Otherwise, local -
ized moments would be exerted that tend to distort the mirror.

different radii and facilitates determination of the sensitivity of a given design to dimensional 
perturbations. Examples of such computations were included in Mehta’s paper. The equations 
derived there are nonlinear and transcendental. He demonstrated that they could be used to 
compute the surface deflections as a function of normalized radius R/a in any meridian and 
find the mount design that provided the minimum rms surface deformation (i.e., figure error) 
given the substrate’s dimensions, Poisson’s ratio, and modulus of rigidity.

mount with symmetrical triangular plates. The two curves represent the deflections along 
the meridian through the inner support point ( θ = 0) and along the meridian at θ = 60° to 
the first. Both methods obviously give the same results. Mehta indicated that the closed-
form solution was computed much more quickly and more economically than the FEA

4.5.3 Counterweighted Mounts
A series of mechanisms with weighted levers can be used to apply force at many discrete 
points on the back of a vertical-axis mirror to support it more or less uniformly in the axial 
direction. Figure 4.22 shows schematically one typical kind of lever back support; it is very 
similar to the one described by Couder (1932). This particular mechanism was designed 
to support the McDonald Observatory’s 107 in. (2.7 m) diameter telescope primary  mirror. 
It was described by Smith (1968). The design provided pointed thrust rods seated at each 
end in conical cups. The points at the ends of the rods were actually polished to a 0.014 in.

hardness. The design pressures on the rod ends were about 200,000 lb/in.2 (1.38 × 103 MPa). 
The cups were packed with silicone grease to prevent contamination.
A major deficiency of counterweight-type mounts is the friction inherent in the bearings. 
The lateral axis of the mechanism shown in Figure 4.22 was originally designed with pin-

4.6 Metrology Mounts
Optical metrology is the subdiscipline of optical engineering in which performance-
related characteristics such as surface figure, radius of curvature, focal length, reflected 
wavefront quality, and modulation transfer function of optical components and systems 
are measured to a high degree of accuracy. These measurements are typically made at 
various in-process stages during manufacture and upon completion of the optics; the 
measured values are compared with previously established standards to determine 
acceptability.
It is frequently not practical to perform these evaluations under the exact conditions of 
final use. A prime example is a large mirror manufactured for eventual use in the gravity-
free environment of space. Even if the mirror is always to remain on Earth, conditions such 
as the direction of the gravity vector during use may be different from those occurring 
during grinding and polishing. The forces normal and tangential to the surface imposed 
during optical finishing must also be taken into consideration since they might be quite 
significant—especially when large-diameter or very thin mirrors are polished by conven -
tional techniques.
The mount used to support a mirror during testing in the final stages of manufacture is 
frequently referred to as a metrology mount. Generally, the mirror’s axis is vertical, and 
its mount must accurately locate the mirror surface relative to the optical test equipment 
and provide a stable, predictable, and repeatable support for the mirror. The metrology 
mount most frequently employed simulates a zero-gravity environment. Typically, such a 
mount supports the mirror at many points so that self-weight deflections from three stra -
tegically located position/orientation defining points and interspan self-weight deflections 
are within specification. As mentioned earlier, Hall (1970) gave a rule-of-thumb criterion for 
the minimum number N of support points needed to prevent self-weight deflections larger 
than specific p–v values during testing. Although not rigorously derived, Hall indicated 
that this criterion, expressed by Equation 4.11, has proved satisfactory for mirrors ranging 
from 40 in. (1 m) diameter × 4 in. (10 cm) thick to 103 in. (2.6 m) diameter × 12 in. (30 cm) 
thick. If the same mount is to be used to support a mirror during polishing as well as dur-
ing testing, the additional loading due to polishing tools and auxiliary weights must be 
considered when computing the number of support points.

A 27-point hydraulic metrology mount for a 70 in. (1.8 m) diameter mirror was described by

4.6.1 36-Point Pneumatic Metrology Mount
Figure  4.26 shows an integrated fabrication/test facility used to manufacture a 150 in. 
(3.8  m) diameter plano-concave mirror. The metrology mount was located between the 
back surface of the mirror and the table of a grinding/polishing machine. This mount 
provided mirror support both during fabrication and during tests. Figure 4.27 shows the 
upper surface of the mount, which was fitted with arrays of 36 circular actuator pads, 
36 rectangular rubber cushion blocks, and 3 race-track-shaped air bearings. Figure 4.28 
shows a closeup view of the pneumatic supports.
During optical tests, the mirror rested on the 36 pads. Three pads from the outer ring 
were deflated, and thin spacers inserted between their pistons and the mirror to serve as 
hard location-defining points. The maximum air pressure was about 8 lb/in.2, slightly dif-
ferent in the two rings, and adjusted so that all 36 pads supported essentially equal parts 
of the mirror weight.
For polishing, the spacers were removed and the mirror floated on all 36 pads. The 
weight of the polishing tool (9000 lb or ~4100 kg) pressed the mirror’s back against the 
36  rubber cushion blocks. Cole pointed out that special precautions were necessary in 
order for the tool weight to be supported equally by the blocks. The rear surface of the mir-
ror and top surface of the table were lapped to match and the blocks ground to the same 
height. To distribute the effects of residual height discrepancies, the mirror was periodi -
cally rotated on the mount. To do this, the mirror was lifted by the three air bearings so 
that it could be moved on a thin layer of air. After rotating, it was lowered back onto the 
blocks and polishing resumed.

the upper surface of the baseplate. These provided mounting surfaces for the axial force

surface of the plate perpendicular to the upper ribs to increase the cross-axis stiffness. The 
force mechanisms, one of which is shown in Figure 4.32, were designed to provide a very 
low spring rate. This low spring rate assured that after each mechanism was adjusted to a

5.1 Introduction and Summary
Although mechanical mounts for large, horizontal- and vertical-axis mirrors are impor -
tant for fixed installations, such as in test equipment associated with mirror fabrication, 
the variable-orientation case presents many more mount design challenges. Optical instru-
ments for astronomical, military, aerospace, and industrial applications that involve large 
mirrors generally require those mirrors to be moved about with respect to the Earth’s 
gravitational field. Much of what was said in Chapters 3 and 4 regarding various radial 
and axial supports in the contexts of horizontal- and vertical-axis mirror applications also 
applies to the variable-orientation mirror. Means for controlling the distribution of forces 
acting on the mirror to minimize optical surface deflection as a function of the  changing 
direction of gravity are described in this chapter. Mirror position–defining systems that 
ensure the retention of mirror-to-telescope alignment also are described. The chapter 
closes with a discussion of techniques for handling large mirrors during fabrication, trans-
port, installation, and maintenance (as required for recoating).

5.3 Defining Systems
Mounts for variable-orientation mirrors maintain the correct shape and position of the 
optical surface within tolerance regardless of the direction of the gravity vector. For 
relatively small, stiff mirrors, a mount providing accurate location is sufficient, because 
gravity-induced deflections can be neglected. Mirrors to be used under gravity release 
conditions in space are also mounted this way, regardless of size (although a metrology 
or gravity off-load mount would be necessary for terrestrial fabrication and testing). Larger 
mirrors require both a stiff defining system that does not overconstrain the mirror and 
another support system that reduces gravity deflection to an acceptable level.
Defining systems are usually based on kinematic principles to avoid overconstraint with 
resulting mirror surface deformation. Because six contacts can uniquely define the loca -
tion of a body in space, most mirror defining systems use six contact points. As discussed 
in Chapter 10 of Volume 1, it is necessary to carefully arrange the contact design to mini -
mize induced forces and moments.
One common arrangement consists of three swivel pads equally spaced on a circle press-
ing against the back of the mirror to control two degrees of freedom (DOFs) in tilt about 
the axes perpendicular to the optical axis as well as location along the optical axis. Two 
more swivel pads pressing against the rim of the mirror provide constraint in translation 
in the plane perpendicular to the optical axis. Finally, a link with ball joints at both ends, 
lying in the neutral plane of the mirror (for a plane parallel mirror, the neutral plane is per-
pendicular to the optical axis and passes through the mirror center of gravity), provides 
constraint against rotation about the optical axis. One end of this link is fixed to the mir -
ror and the other to the mirror cell. Moments and forces induced by this arrangement are 
limited to those from residual friction in the swivels and ball joints. A defining system of 
this type is used for all types of mirrors, even if the mirror rim is not a cylinder.
As discussed in Chapter 1 on mirror performance specifications, the tolerable forces and 
moments for the defining system are obtained from a unit force and moment analysis. Of 
particular concern are the moments applied to the mirror since forces in a kinematic defin-
ing system will simply displace the mirror without changing the optical figure. Defining 
points usually must be large enough to reduce stress to an acceptable level; at the same 
time, the mounting pad coplanarity problem (flatness tolerance of the defining areas) imposes 
the need for additional rotational DOFs within the defining point. The most common solu-
tion is a swivel pad using a ball-and-socket joint, or a multi-DOF rotational flexure.
Equations for flexure design are given in Chapter 10 of Volume 1 on kinematics and 
the use of flexures in opto-mechanical applications. For defining systems, the moment 
necessary to cause breakaway in a ball and socket must also be calculated, because this 
moment influences mirror figure. Breakaway is defined as the minimum moment neces -

Static friction coefficients in ball-and-socket mounts are typically high on the order of 
unity. Additional sources of both friction and hysteresis are dust shields on commercial 
ball joints and tie rod ends (Barr et al., 1983). It is often necessary to remove these shields 
(Barr et al., 1985) or use unshielded bearings to minimize hysteresis. Some mirror mounts

breakaway torques or moments are often undesirably high. See Design Example 5.1.
Load cells are often incorporated into defining points used for large mirrors to deter -
mine the actual force exerted on the mirror. Most large mirror support systems are astatic 
and deliver forces that are relatively independent of position. The defining points can then 
be used to adjust mirror position without changing optical surface figure. However, the 
load cells provide feedback to insure that excessive moments and forces are not applied 
to the mirror. In addition, the load cells are used to limit slewing speeds. If the mirror 
is rotated too quickly, dynamic forces may become large enough to unload the defining 
points. This can result in a change in mirror position.
Multipoint whiffletree mounts do not require a defining system because the stiffness of 
the whiffletree is much higher than that of a flotation-type mount. Adjustment of a mirror 
mounted on a whiffletree is accomplished by moving the entire whiffletree assembly. For 
a right circular cylinder mirror, the whiffletree assembly is normally divided into three 
radially symmetric parts. By moving each of the three whiffletrees, the mirror is adjusted 
in two DOFs of tilt (axes perpendicular to the optical axis) and in piston (along the opti -
cal axis). Examples are the whiffletree mounts for the mirror segments of the Keck and 
GTC telescopes. Each segment is mounted on a 36-point whiffletree, and each whiffletree 
is divided into three 12-point assemblies. The latter assemblies are moved in a direction 
parallel to the optical axis to adjust the segment position.

5.4 Multipoint Supports
5.4.1 Introduction
As stated in the discussion of defining points, large variable-orientation mirrors typically 
require a defining system to accurately locate the position of the mirror with respect to 
the rest of the optical system and a support system to minimize self-weight deflection. For 
some applications (particularly smaller, stiff mirrors), it is possible to combine these two 
functions either in a kinematic bipod mount or a more elaborate multipoint whiffletree 
mounting. Larger mirrors used in astronomical telescopes usually require both a defining 
system and a flotation type of support. The flotation support provides a distribution of 
force against the back and sides of the mirror to minimize self-weight deflection. Flotation 
supports are either passive or active. Active supports provide variable forces to correct 
deflection of the mirror from a variety of sources such as temperature gradients and struc-
tural hysteresis. Although real-time metrology schemes are used as part of the control 
loop for active systems, if the deflection is sufficiently repeatable, control of the active sup-
ports can be from a lookup table.
Self-weight deflection is described in several different ways: maximum peak-to-valley 
(p–v), rms, or in terms of optical aberration. Maximum deflection is most common in older 
discussions of self-weight deflection and represents the maximum deviation of the optical 
surface from a stress-free form. The rms is an average of the deflection across the entire opti-
cal surface. Therefore, it is considered to be a better index of deflection since it more accu-
rately represents the entire surface. In contrast, maximum or p–v deflection can be skewed 
by a single sharp point of deflection, which may not strongly influence optical performance. 
The shape of the deflected surface can be fit to a best-fit sphere or parabola; for a concave 
astronomical telescope mirror, a simple change in radius resulting from deflection is usually 
acceptable. Finally, the deflection can be described in terms of aberrations, most commonly 
using Zernike polynomials (see Doyle et al., 2012). Removing the focus polynomial from the 
description of the deflected surface provides a more accurate representation of that surface 
because focus is adjusted in an astronomical telescope. It is important to recall in any dis -
cussion of mirror deflection that there is a factor of 2 between surface and wavefront errors.
5.4.2 Historical Background
Passive flotation systems were most commonly employed as supports for astronomical 
telescopes built through 1976, including the Russian BTA-6 telescope (Bolshoi Teleskop Alt-
azimutalnyi, which translates as large altazimuth telescope). In 1979, the original six-mirror 
multimirror telescope (MMT) and 3.8 m United Kingdom Infrared Telescope (UKIRT) 
were commissioned. Both were pioneers in using a combination of passive and active con-
trols. The 3.6 m European Southern Observatory New Technology Telescope (ESO NTT), 
commissioned in 1989, further developed active supports. Although the individual mirror 
segments are supported on passive whiffletrees, the 10 m Keck, commissioned in 1992, 
uses an active control of the position of the individual segments. The current generation 
of 8 m class telescopes, including the Subaru, ESO VLT, and Gemini, all employ elaborate 
active multipoint supports.
Even with active control of the individual supports, self-weight deflection of a mirror is 
determined by the number and overall layout of the supports. As telescope sizes grew, the 
design of multipoint support systems for large mirrors became increasingly important. 
For example, the 1.8 m telescope built by Rosse in 1845 employed a 1.8 m speculum metal 

=== PAGE 207 ===
183Mounting Large, Variable-Orientation Mirrors
mirror that was 125 mm thick. Self-weight deflection of this mirror was minimized by an 
81-point mounting (Rosse, 1850). In the absence of modern elastic theory, Rosse apparently 
developed the support for his mirror by experiment.
Pioneering work on the support of large optics was done by Couder in 1930 (Couder, 
1931). Lacking digital computers, Couder used elastic theory coupled with experiment to 
develop engineering models for the support of large mirrors.
Couder showed that, according to classical plate theory, self-weight deflection of mirror 
scales is d4/h2. This assumes constant support geometry, no change in material, that d is 
the mirror diameter and that h is the mirror thickness. This scaling relationship is some -
times called Couder’s law in the mirror design literature (Wilson, 1999). Couder found 
that the optimum support ring diameter minimizing self-weight deflection is about 0.667 
of the mirror diameter. This is in good agreement with modern analysis. He also showed 
that, as mirror size grew, a single support ring was no longer sufficient to keep self-weight 
deflection within acceptable optical performance tolerances. Further, multiple concentric 
rings of support were necessary. Couder also was able to estimate the amount of aberra -
tion introduced by different types of supports.

Because increased pad size reduces self-weight deflection, most multipoint support sys-
tems use the largest possible pads, up to a/b ≈ 0.6. Two design issues with increased mount-
ing pad size are contact stress and thermal stress. Contact stress results from poor contact 
between the pad and mirror back surface. Providing two axes of rotation in each pad, using 
a ball-and-socket joint or gimbal, allows the pad to rotate into full contact with the back of 
the mirror. It is also common to grind the mirror back flat (at least locally) to further reduce 
contact stress between the mirror and the mounting pads. It is possible to approximate a 
large pad surface by using three smaller contact pads on a common diameter, limiting con-
tact stress. This type of load spreader is used on the LBT and Subaru mirrors.
Thermal stresses are induced from differences in thermal coefficient of expansion in pad 
and mirror, when the pad is attached to the back surface of the mirror. Usually, the pad is 
made of a metal with a different thermal coefficient of expansion from that of the mirror. If 
the mirror simply rests on the pad, then slippage occurs between the two surfaces when the 
temperature changes. This slippage limits the thermal stress to a small value. However, if 
the pad is bonded to the mirror back, significant stress is induced with changes in tempera-
ture. Thermal stress in bonded contacts is discussed in Chapter 11 of Volume 1 on analysis.
Some telescope mirror mounts combine axial and radial supports, typically by insert -
ing both supports into pockets in the back of the mirror. The support pockets are deep 
enough to reach the middle or neutral plane of the mirror, thereby minimizing self-weight-
induced astigmatism in the horizontal-axis position. Examples include the 5 m Hale, 6 m 
Russian BTA-6, and 8 m Subaru. The Keck and GTC telescopes use radial supports in 
central pockets in each segment, with axial support provided by a 36-point whiffletree.
Over time, even a relatively low stress induced by the action of the support in such a 
pocket can cause failure in the mirror material by static fatigue. Usually, the support is 
bonded to the pocket. A low-coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) material such as Invar 
may be used to minimize thermal stress in combination with low-CTE mirror materials. 
The Hale primary support is an example. Alternately, thermal stress is reduced by using 
radially compliant flexures between the mirror and support. Both Subaru and Keck use 
this design approach.

The use of buttons or bosses made of low-expansion metal such as Invar 36 bonded with 
adhesive to the backs or rims of mirrors made of fused silica, ULE, or Zerodur is common 
practice as a means for attaching mechanical supports. Figure 5.18 shows several types of 
such bosses. Some are bonded into holes ground into the mirror substrates, while others 
are bonded externally to mirror surfaces. The bosses shown in Figure 5.18b have flexures 
to minimize transfer of moments into the mirror because such moments could distort the 
optical surface.
Many mirrors use bosses attached to their rims as attachment points for flexures. Typical 
concepts for mountings needing this feature are shown in Figures 9.35 and 9.44 of Volume 1. 
Circular mirrors might have bonded-on bosses of the type shown in Figure 5.19. The mirror 
for which this particular boss was designed was 15.0 in. (38.1 cm) in diameter, so the bond-
ing surface of the boss was shaped cylindrically with a nominal radius, the same as that of 
the mirror rim. The flexure designed to mate with this boss is shown in Figure 5.20 . It was 
angled so that it could be attached to a flat pad on the ID of a cylindrical structure. Note the 
square hole at the interface between the two components. It was intended to maintain an 
angular alignment between the mating parts.
Rectangular mirrors, with or without cells, may also be attached to structures with flex-
ures as in Figure 9.42 of Volume 1. Possible designs for the bosses and flexures for such 
mounts are shown in Figures 5.21 and 5.22. The flexures in this case are straight because 
the mirror edges are nominally parallel to the structure surfaces to which those flexures
