Two different definitions are used to separate large from small mirrors; one based on opti-
cal surface deflection (which affects image quality) and the other on means for handling. 
A mirror is considered large if a complex support system is necessary to reduce self-weight 
deflection to an acceptable level. Alternatively, a mirror is considered large if one person 
cannot pick it up and move it from one place to another.
Although mirror mass and weight are often used interchangeably in discussions of mir-
ror design, the meanings of these words are different. Mass is resistance to acceleration 
and is independent of gravity. It is most often used in reduced or zero-gravity applications, 
such as in outer space or in dynamic applications. On the other hand, weight is force. It 
applies to all mirrors as they are fabricated on Earth and when used in terrestrial applica -
tions. Self-weight equals mirror mass times the local acceleration g of the Earth’s gravity. 
Nominally, g = 9.807 m/s 2. Calculating mirror structural deflection requires evaluation 
of the force on the mirror. This force is normally called self-weight. In the International 
System of Units (SI), the unit of mass is the kg, while the force unit is the Newton (which 
is 1  kg times 1 m/s 2 of acceleration). It is customary to express mirror weight also in 
kgf, which makes for confusion with units when calculating self-weight deflection. In 
this chapter, we are concerned primarily with cases where weight is the appropriate 
terminology.
It is desirable to reduce the weights of large mirrors for practically all applications. 
Satellite launch capability is limited and costly so minimization of weight of telescopes 
intended for use in space is essential. According to Meinel (1981), the costs of large ter -
restrial telescopes are closely associated with overall weights of those systems. Because 
the primary mirror is the largest single-item weight in a telescope and drives the total 
weight, reducing the primary mirror weight tends to reduce cost. Key figures of merit 
for mirror weight include areal density and lightweight efficiency. The thermal time 
constants of large solid mirrors are prohibitively long in some situations, as discussed in 
Chapter 1. In this chapter, we describe how these constants can be reduced by using vari -
ous types of lightweight configurations and by providing ventilation. We also consider 
how the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of the mirror material and its unifor -
mity can influence large mirror response to conventional thermal soak or to cryogenic 
applications.
Large mirrors are made in a variety of configurations. Thin mirrors, made in either flat-
back or meniscus shapes, reduce mirror weight, lower cost, and minimize thermal time 
constant. Simply reducing thickness, which increases aspect ratio (diameter/thickness or 
D/t), lowers stiffness and makes mirror support more complex by making the location of 
the center of gravity (CG) inaccessible in conventional mirror mounts. Mirror design is 
therefore almost always a compromise.

of candidate structural constructions, surface contours, materials, and mount configura -
tions. This menu is shown in Figure 2.1. The path indicated by arrows is one possible

can be made from the items listed here is obviously quite large. Representative configu -
rations are shown in Figure 2.2. In order to select the optimum combination, one must 
consider many performance-related requirements, such as the magnitude and distribu -
tion of allowable surface figure errors at various possible orientations under self-weight, 
gravity-free, or acceleration conditions; thermal inputs and losses, including gradients; 
coating spectral reflectance and durability; microroughness contributions to scatter; radi -

2.2.5 Thermal Figures of Merit
Temperature changes can induce deflections in large mirrors that are comparable in 
magnitude to gravity deflections. The effect of temperature changes is aggravated by the

long thermal time constant of most large mirrors, leading to significant gradient effects. 
A recent concern is local air turbulence effects on optical propagation near the mirror due 
to temperature differences between the surrounding air and mirror surface. Uniformity 
of thermal expansion is an issue with very large mirrors and mirrors used at cryogenic 
temperatures.
Thermal time constants are discussed in detail in Chapter 1 on mirror performance 
specifications. Here, the discussion of thermal time constants is limited to effects on large 
mirrors. The Mt. Wilson anecdote illustrates that the thermal time constant of a large 
mirror is typically longer than the rate of change of temperature in the service environ -
ment. If the temperature of the mirror lags behind that of the environment, a temperature 
gradient is induced in the mirror. This will distort the optical surface. Long thermal time 
constants are also an issue in mirror fabrication, requiring long waits for the mirror to 
reach equilibrium before testing to insure that the presence of thermal gradients does not 
affect the optical figure. Reducing the thermal time constant of large mirror is therefore 
highly desirable.

Mirror seeing became important with increased demands for higher resolution terres -

would be small, ~0.06 arc second.
Right after sunset, the air temperature around a telescope mirror changes rapidly, by 
as much as 10 K/h in some locations. Following this rate of change is an extremely chal -
lenging problem for mirror thermal control systems. For most observatory locations, the 
rate of air temperature changes during the rest of the night is much slower, usually on the 
order of 0.25 K/h. Even this relatively slow change in temperature is a difficult thermal 
control problem for massive, solid mirrors. According to Bohhanan et al. (2000), experience 
with the Mt. Wilson telescope led to efforts toward matching the mirror and air tempera -
ture. Many contemporary observatory telescope mirrors are cooled during the day to an 
approximation of the predicted mean night air temperature.
In the mid-1980s, Angel proposed active control of mirror temperature by forced air 
ventilation of the inside of lightweight honeycomb or sandwich mirrors. To reduce the 
thermal time constant of the mirror to less than 1 h, all parts must be thin, about 25 mm 
thick. Maintaining adequate stiffness with thin sections led to the use of a sandwich struc-
ture. Previous sandwich mirrors were expensive to produce. Angel also developed the 
novel form of spin casting to near net shape using borosilicate glass in a rotating furnace 
to reduce cost. This process is discussed in Section 2.3.9
The thermal expansion of borosilicate glass is relatively high, about 3.3 × 10 −6 K−1 when 
compared to the zero expansion materials such as ULE or Zerodur. However, active control 
of temperature gradients and mirror figure would offset thermal changes in the mirror.
Initial experiments in forced air cooling of sample borosilicate mirrors showed that ther-
mal coupling between the air and mirror surface was poor, requiring high flow rates to 
maintain mirror temperature near ambient air temperature. These results led to modified 
ventilation schemes, with careful control of the temperature of the air used to ventilate 
the mirror interior. For a typical mirror section, a flow of about 8.3 L/s of temperature-
conditioned air is required (Cheng and Angel, 1988).
In practice, developing good thermal control of large lightweight borosilicate mirrors 
has proven to be difficult and time consuming. The 6.5 m MMT M1 mirror telescope was 
commissioned in 2000 with a lightweight spin-cast sandwich mirror of the type proposed 
by Angel. However, the goal of maintaining the mirror temperature within ±0.2 K was 
not achieved until 2009 and then only for part of the night. Considerable development

mirror surface optical coating heater to bring the optical surface into thermal equilibrium 
with the ambient air temperature. Feeding an electrical current through an aluminum 
surface coating provides the heating.
As mirrors become larger, and as operating temperature ranges become more extreme, 
the isotropy of the CTE of the mirror material is of increasing importance. Low-CTE 
mirror materials, active figure control, and thermal control can minimize distortions of 
the mirror surface induced by temperature gradients and local atmospheric turbulence 
effects. A spatial variation (or anisotropy) in CTE induces a change in mirror figure with 
temperature that may not respond to these means of control.
There is limited knowledge in the literature on both the magnitude and distribution 
of spatial variation of CTE in mirrors (see Pellerin et al., 1985). Anisotropy of 3%–5% in 
CTE has been suggested as a rough rule of thumb. Measurement of a 1.9 m diameter Cer-

through the 300 mm thickness. According to Osterbrock (1993), about 95% of a series of 
measurements of the anisotropy of Schott Zerodur are within 15 × 10 −9 K−1. The CTE of 
Corning 7972 ULE measured using an ultrasonic technique that is accurate to ±2 ppb/°C 
(Sabia et al., 2006) indicated the anisotropy for large mirror blanks such as those for the 
Gemini telescopes to be about ±5 × 10 −9 K−1. Krim et al. (1990) reported that, in thermal 
analysis of ULE, a spatial variation of about ±15 × 10−9 K−1 is commonly assumed. More 
recent measurements of actual large mirror blanks suggest that this assumption for ULE 
is overly conservative.

2.3 Historical Perspective
2.3.1 Speculum Metal Mirrors
The first large telescope mirrors (such as Herschel’s 1.2 m and Rosse’s 1.8 m) were made of 
speculum metal. This is a copper–tin alloy with a reflectivity of about 66% in the visible 
when polished. Per Tolansky and Donaldson (1947), a drawback to speculum metal is tar-
nishing when exposed to moist air, causing a loss of 2%–10% in reflectivity over 6 months. 
Mirrors made of this metal had to be polished periodically to remove this tarnishing. This 
posed a risk of loss of optical figure.
In the mid-1850s, silver-on-glass films were developed that could be applied using chem-
ical means. Reflectivity of a silver film in the visible is high in comparison with speculum, 
with a fresh chemically deposited silver film reflecting about 95% at 550 nm. Like specu -
lum metal, silver reflecting films tarnish in service, with reflectivity falling to about 77%. 
Unlike speculum, the silver film can be chemically stripped and replaced without the risk 
of changing the mirror figure.
2.3.2 Cast Glass Mirrors
Silver-on-glass films were used in the next generation of telescope mirrors. These were 
made as thick solid right-circular cylinders from crown (plate) glasses. Thermal inertia and 
the resulting temperature gradient–induced distortion was a serious issue. One example 
of a thick crown glass mirror is the 2.5 m primary for the Hooker telescope on Mt. Wilson. 
It had problems with thermal inertia as discussed in Chapter 1.
Better thermal performance was obtained from the use of Corning Pyrex, which was 
invented in 1915 and first used in the 5 m Hale telescope (see Florence, 1995). The CTE 
of the special type of Pyrex used in this mirror is 2.3 × 10 −6 K−1, which is a factor of three 
smaller than that of crown glass. Thermal inertia of the Hale telescope primary mirror was 
minimized by the use of a lightweight cast open-back structure (see Bowen, 1960). A series 
of similar mirrors were used in the next generation of large telescopes, including the Lick 
Observatory 3 m and Kitt Peak National Observatory 2.1 m. Vacuum-deposited aluminum 
reflective films replaced silver on the optical surfaces of this generation of large optics. 
Although the deposition process was more complex, involving high vacuum, the long-term 
durability of the aluminum film was superior to that of silver. First light for the Hale tele-
scope occurred in December 1947 after several years of work interruption by World War II. 
The telescope’s productivity continues today. Most large modern telescopes are designed to 
be recoated in situ or on-site in order to maintain usefulness over extended lifetimes.
2.3.3 Low-Expansion Mirror Materials
During the early 1960s, Owens Illinois developed a near-zero CTE glass-ceramic, Cer-Vit 
C-101, for use in telescope mirrors. Smoothness and thermal stability of this material were 
discussed by Dietz and Bennett (1967). About the same time, Corning was able to produce 

=== BOOK PAGE 87 ===
63Large Mirror Design
fused silica mirror blanks up to a diameter of 4 m. These new low-CTE materials were used 
in a new generation of large telescopes, with primary mirror diameters up to 4 m. Thermal 
gradients were not a serious problem with near-zero CTE materials, so mirrors were made 
in the form of massive solid right-circular cylinders. The primary mirror for the Mayall 
telescope at Kitt Peak National Observatory, commissioned in 1973, is an example of this 
type of mirror. It has a diameter of 4 m and edge thickness of 0.57 m. Half a dozen tele -
scopes using massive low-CTE primary mirrors of this type, with diameters ranging from 
3.6 to 4 m, were commissioned between 1973 and 1979. One feature of the mirrors used in 
these telescopes is a relatively large central perforation for Cassegrain applications. This 
hole is normally about one-third of the mirror diameter in size.
A telescope with a massive conventional solid mirror was the BTA-6 located at the 
Crimean Observatory in what was then the Soviet Union. When commissioned in 1976, 
this telescope was the largest in the world. Its primary mirror was a meniscus, with a 6 m 
diameter and 600 mm thickness. Unfortunately, the quality of the borosilicate glass used 
in the primary mirror was low and the condition of the mirror surface was poor. Because 
the mirror was made of a borosilicate glass similar to Corning Pyrex and was not light -
weighted, it suffered from a long thermal time constant. An ambient temperature change 
of 2 K would put the telescope out of service for 2–3 days.
The introduction by Schott Glaswerke of low-expansion Zerodur glass ceramic to the 
realm of astronomical telescopes took the form of the 3.6 m diameter by 0.59 m thick pri -
mary for a telescope on Celar Alto in Spain in the early 1970s. This material has since 
become a material of choice for many astronomical instruments including the Keck tele -
scopes and orbiting x-ray telescopes such as ROSAT and Chandra.
According to Meinel (1978), studies of the then current generation of astronomical tele -
scopes showed that their cost varied roughly as a power of the primary mirror diameter, 
with the exponent typically being between 2.6 and 2.8. Extrapolating this power law scal -
ing showed that the next-generation large astronomical telescopes, with primary mirrors 
8–10 m in diameter, would be unaffordable. This was because costs would be 6–10 times 
higher than 4 m class instruments. An important assumption in these cost studies was that 
the existing technology would be used in the new instruments. Concerns about cost led to 
the development of new mirror technologies.
2.3.4 Thickness Reduction
Reducing mirror thickness was the first approach to reducing large mirror cost. The 3.8 m 
United Kingdom Infrared Telescope (UKIRK), commissioned in 1979, was a pioneer with 
an aspect ratio of about 13. Even more radical was the ESO NTT with a 3.5 m diameter pri-
mary mirror having an aspect ratio of about 15 (see Tarengi, 1986). Other new technologies 
used in the NTT were an altazimuth mounting and closed-loop, active control of the pri -
mary mirror optical figure. Active control of the mirror figure compensated for structural 
flexure and thermal effects.
Success of the NTT led to even higher aspect ratios of about 40 being used in the primary 
mirrors for the Subaru, Gemini, and ESO VLT 8 m telescopes. A constant thickness menis-
cus shape minimizes issues from temperature gradients and also simplifies the support 
system design. Active control of the mirror optical surface figure further compensates for 
any deformations of the mirror. Low-CTE materials such as Zerodur and ULE are used in 
these large mirrors to reduce thermal distortion. In these telescopes, actuators deform the 
large meniscus mirrors to maintain their correct shapes; this is a relatively complex control 
problem, but one that yielded to intense development efforts.

2.3.8 Segmented Mirrors
Another approach to building large mirrors uses thin segments to make up the primary mir-
ror such as those for the two Keck telescopes. In contrast to a large full-aperture thin menis-
cus mirror, the individual segments are relatively stiff, and the control problem, although 
still formidable, is reduced to maintaining the rigid body position of the segments relative to 
each other. The individual segments typically are made in the form of hexagons. Optical fab-
rication of the segments in the required quantities is a challenging problem and was solved 
through the use of an innovative stressed mirror polishing technique to make segments for 
the first Keck telescope (see Lubliner and Nelson, 1980; Nelson et al., 1980). This technique 
was explained by Mast and Nelson (1990). A report on the success of that manufacturing 
process was given by Pepi (1990). Figure 2.25 illustrates the technique for bending the mirror 
blank symmetrically during polishing. For both Keck 10 m telescopes, the hexagons are 1.9 m 
across and 75 mm thick and have with aspect ratios of about 25. Other segmented mirror 
telescopes include the Gran Telescopio Canarias (GTC) 10.2 m telescope in the Canary Islands 
and the South African Large Telescope (SALT) 9.2 m telescope in South Africa. According to 
Sporer (2006) and by Daniel et al. (2010), it is expected that the segments for a Thirty Meter 
Telescope (TMT) will be made in the United States using this basic technique.
2.3.9 Spin-Cast Mirrors
Currently, the most successful approach to making one-piece large mirrors is spin casting 
borosilicate honeycomb mirrors. These mirrors are much stiffer than high-aspect-ratio menis-

CTE of borosilicate glass requires a complex thermal control system, as well as active sup-
port of the primary mirror. The first large spin-cast borosilicate mirror placed in service 
was the 6.5 m upgrade of the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory’s Multiple Mirror 
Telescope (MMT) on Mount Hopkins, in Arizona (see Olbert et al., 1994).

2.4 Large Mirror Design Process
Mirror design is a three-step process comprising predesign, first-order design, and final 
design analysis. Any mirror design starts with performance specifications. In addition to 
optical performance parameters such as radius of curvature, diameter, and conic constant, 
mechanical parameters are also specified. These include self-weight deflection and total 
weight. Self-weight deflection is normally part of a larger error budget including ther -
mal distortion and fabrication errors. For dynamic applications, self-weight deflection is 
derived from the relationship between frequency and static deflection.
Scaling from previous designs is a common technique used for initial analysis. * Mirror 
weight can be predicted using scaling laws such as those developed by Valente (1990). † 
Alternatively, weight is estimated from areal density. A conservative areal density value 
for lightweight mirrors is 180 kg/m2, which is represented by the Hubble Space Telescope 
(HST) primary mirror. A state-of-the-art value is about 15 kg/m2 and is represented by the 
primary mirror for the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). Application of simple self-
weight deflection equations is another way to estimate mirror performance rapidly. These 
equations are discussed in Chapter 1.
First-order design is the detailed design of the mirror using closed-form or “hand cal -
culations.” In reality, the nonlinear nature of the detailed equations governing mirror 
self-weight deflection and stiffness makes use of computer aid highly desirable. During 
this phase of the design, the stiffness and self-weight of the mirror are determined using 
parametric analysis. The present chapter provides the basic equations necessary for first-
order design. It is possible to develop a mirror design using only first-order analysis. This 
practice was common before the widespread application of finite element analysis (FEA).
After first-order design, the candidate mirror configuration is subjected to detailed analy-
sis using FEA. There are often large differences in estimated performance between FEA 
and first-order analysis. The latter serves as a good check for preliminary FEA. Good agree-
ment is usually defined as agreement between these results within about 20%. Final design 
is guided by the FEA, although it may be necessary to iterate between the parametric first-
order analysis and the FEA to achieve an optimal design. Skipping first-order analysis and 
going directly to FEA is often unproductive in that it can take a long time to converge to a 
design solution using just FEA. How to use FEA is outside the scope of this book. A good 
introduction to the use of FEA in mirror design analysis is provided in Doyle et al. (2012).
2.5 Design of Thin Mirrors
2.5.1 Thin, Flat Back Mirrors
A thin mirror is defined as one with an aspect ratio (diameter/thickness) greater than the 
classical 6:1. This definition is somewhat imprecise because the aspect ratios of a number 
of large classical telescope primary mirrors exceed 6:1. For example, the aspect ratio of 
the primary mirror of the Hooker 2.5 m telescope is about 8:1. Generally, a mirror with an 
aspect ratio of 10:1 or more is considered thin.

Thin mirrors are used to reduce cost, improve thermal equilibrium time, and lower 
overall system weight. Availability of material for the primary mirror also affects thick -
ness. Some fabrication methods limit the thickness of the mirror, with large aspect ratios 
required for larger mirrors.
The sizes of current amateur and professional telescopes overlap, with many amateur 
telescope primary mirror diameters exceeding 500 mm (19.7 in.). In the recent past, instru-

borosilicate glasses such as Pyrex are used in the primary mirrors of most of the larger 
amateur telescopes. Kriege and Berry (2001) indicated that, because the thicknesses of 
borosilicate glass blanks are generally limited to 54 mm (2.13 in.), the aspect ratios of ama-
teur mirrors of 300–625 mm (11.8–24.6 in.) diameter range from 5.6 to 11.6.
Modern telescopes are made with relatively fast primary focal ratios, typically about 
f/2. Since the radius of curvature of a mirror with a fast focal ratio is relatively short, the 
sagittal depth is large. The combination of a fast focal ratio and thin mirror leads to a large 
change in thickness of the mirror from center to edge. For instance, with a focal ratio of 
f/2, the thickness varies by a factor of two from center to edge when the mirror aspect 
ratio is about 16:1. The limit on aspect ratio is set by the sagittal depth. When the sagittal 
depth equals the mirror edge thickness, the center thickness is zero. Further reductions in 
the focal ratio create a hole in the center of the mirror; the sagittal depth then exceeds the 
mirror thickness. The maximum aspect ratio for a given focal ratio is given approximately 
by ζ = 16 ( fno). This relationship is accurate to better than 2% for focal ratios as fast as f/1.

Because self-weight deflection scales as the square of the aspect ratio, the stiffnesses of 
thin mirrors are smaller than those of solid mirrors with the classical aspect ratio of ζ = 6.

the large variation in thickness for a thin mirror with a fast focal ratio.
The mirror thermal time constant is proportional to the square of the mirror thickness.

fast focal ratios. For a thin mirror with ζ = 12 and fno of 2, the mirror center thickness is 
about 62% of the edge thickness. Therefore, the thermal time constant at the mirror cen -

radial direction increases the magnitudes of thermal gradients in the mirror when the 
temperature changes. Thus, increased sensitivity to temperature gradients is an inherent 
drawback of thin, flat-back mirrors.

2.5.2 Thin Meniscus Mirrors
Meniscus mirrors are defined as curved mirrors with constant thickness. For constant 
thickness, the magnitude of the radius of curvature of the back of the mirror must be 
greater than that of the front by an amount equivalent to the center thickness of the mirror. 
If the radius of curvature at the front and back are the same, there will be slight change 
in thickness from center to edge. Thin meniscus mirrors are defined as meniscus mirrors 
when the ratio of diameter to central thickness is greater than about six. This definition is 
imprecise for the same reasons as for a thin, flat-back mirror.
Like the thin, flat-back mirror, thin meniscus mirrors are used to reduce cost, lower 
weight, and minimize thermal effects. Because the meniscus mirror does not vary in

Meinel (1971) indicated that single-arch mirrors offer minimum mass telescope designs 
when combined with Meinel type structures in Cassegrain telescopes. In this configura -

with the primary baffle forming part of the structure. Vanes connect the primary to the 
secondary baffle. Single-arch mirrors with Meinel structures are used in several space 
telescopes such as the 850 mm Spitzer Space Telescope.
According to Anderson et  al. (1982), the self-weight deformation of the single-arch 
mirror when subjected to gravity normal to the mirror surface, that is, parallel to the 
optical axis, is almost entirely radial and produces primarily focus shift. Gravity deflec -
tion of the single arch is easy to predict (see Steir et al., 1998). By testing the mirror face 
up and face down, it is possible to subtract out gravity deflections. This type of testing 
is relatively straightforward because the center hub mirror support is stiff and sim -
ple. Thermal response of the single arch also is simple; a radial temperature gradient 
produces mostly focus. Telkamp and Applewhite (1992) showed that a simple central 
heater can provide a highly reliable means of adjusting focus in a space telescope with 
a single-arch primary.
Materials used for single-arch mirrors include beryllium, aluminum, metal matrix com-
posites, as well as low-CTE glasses and glass ceramics. Single-arch mirrors in diameters 
up to about 1.5 m are used in terrestrial observatories (see Carter, 1972). Single-arch mir -

Camera, 400 mm for the STAR-LITE, and 850 mm for the Spitzer. The latter is an example 
of an ultra-lightweight single arch. It has an outer diameter of 850 mm, a central hole diam-
eter of 290 mm, and a central thickness of 40 mm. Weight of the mirror is 14 kg (30.8 in.). 
The mirror material is O-30 beryllium.

2.7 Structured Lightweight Mirrors
2.7.1 Sandwich Mirrors
A mirror lightweighted with a cellular or a cored cross section is structurally more efficient 
than its equivalent-sized solid version. Mirrors of this type are frequently called honeycomb 
structures. Provision of axial supports (parallel to the axis), radial supports (normal to the 
axis), and defining supports pose major design issues. Some designs have the supporting 
forces applied within the interior of the substrate at or near the neutral surface where grav-
itational moments of the localized volumes are balanced. Gross material density near the 
neutral surface of the mirror that contributes little to bending stiffness is usually reduced 
by lightweighting. Shear deflections are then increased. They may then partially offset the 
bending stiffness improvement. Since mirror weight is reduced, the net stiffness-to-weight 
ratio generally improves.
Five basic built-up structures used for lightweight cellular mirrors were shown in 
Figure 2.2. In some designs, the core (here frequently referred to as the web) is integral 
with the front face sheet and unneeded material is machined away. In others, the core is 
made as a separate part and completely or partially attached to those sheets. Attachment 
means for the latter types of construction include fusion bonding, frit bonding, adhesive 
bonding, and (for metal mirrors) brazing and welding. The geometric parameters avail -
able to the designer for lightweighting a mirror are material characteristics; face-sheet 
thickness; rib height, spacing, and thickness; and cell size and pattern. Referring back to 
Figure 2.4, we see in views (b) through (d) the three commonly used cell patterns for most 
cellular mirrors. View (a) of that figure shows the key cross-sectional dimensions of any 
cell in the models. These dimensions are as follows: tF is the front face-sheet thickness, tFB is 
the back face-sheet thickness, tW is the core wall thickness, hC is the core height, and NA is 
the distance from the front of the mirror to the neutral surface.

2.7.2 Open-Back Configurations

perpendicular ribs forming open pockets or cells in the back of the mirror. Pocket geometry

Holes are sometimes placed in the junctions of the ribs in the shear core of an open-back 
mirror. This practice reduces the overall stiffness of the mirror by interrupting the conti -
nuity of the ribs (see Schwessinger, 1968). These holes are often introduced as a means of 
reducing weight. Any weight saving is usually offset by increased thickness of rib mate -
rial around the hole. Another reason for holes in the rib intersections is to reduce wall 
thicknesses in order to reduce their thermal time constants. There are also comparable 
thickness areas in the junctions of the ribs with the face sheet. These holes do not affect the 
thermal time constants in these areas.

For these new large space optical systems, two new technologies were envisioned. These

Using Bely’s stiffness criteria from Table 2.2, the stiffness parameter of the proposed 
ultra-lightweight mirrors would be between 10 −6 and 10 −9 m, requiring 10–100 actuators 
per square meter. Such low stiffness means that conventional passive supports are imprac-
tical. Therefore, the optical figure must be sensed and actively controlled. Mirrors of this 
type are sometimes called semirigid since their stiffnesses are higher than those of small 
deformable mirrors used for fast correction of optical wave front errors induced by atmo -
spheric seeing and other effects. An important discriminator is that the bandwidth of the 
control system associated with semirigid mirrors is on the order of a few Hertz, versus 
hundreds of Hertz for deformable mirrors.
In 1988, even before the launch of the HST in 1990, DARPA in the United States began the 
LAMP at ITEK (see Plante, 1991). LAMP was intended to demonstrate new lightweight mir-
ror technology using very thin face sheets combined with active optical figure control. The 
mirror embodied several new technologies: a very thin face sheet with active figure control, 
segmented design, and a graphite epoxy backup or reaction structure. Mirror diameter for 
the LAMP was 4 m, with an f/1.25 focal ratio. Seven mirror segments made up the mirror; 
one central hexagonal segment surrounded by six petal-shaped segments (see Figure 2.3). 
Corning ULE was used for the face sheets, which were only 17 mm thick. Each segment 
rested on an array of fine figure actuators. These in turn were connected to a stiff graphite 
epoxy backup structure. The individual segments were positioned relative to each other 
using active bipods. Although advancing large active mirror technology, the LAMP areal 
density of about 140 kg/m2 was not a large reduction over that of the HST (ρA ≈ 180 kg/m2).
A similar mirror demonstration project was the Adaptive Large Optics Technology 
(ALOT) also developed at ITEK and completed in 1994 (see Cox and Ferber, 1996). ALOT 
was a 4 m, f/1.7 circular segmented mirror. With a central circular segment and six sur -
rounding petal-shaped segments, the configuration of ALOT was similar to that of LAMP . 
Unlike LAMP , the ALOT demonstration comprised only the central segment and one petal. 
All segments were open-back, machined lightweight mirror structures made of ULE, con-
nected using fine figure actuators to a graphite epoxy composite reaction structure. Each 
actuator used a combination of a coarse lead screw and a fine control with an electro -
static positioner. The ranges of travel and resolution of the coarse motion were 1.5 mm and 
170 nm, while the range of travel of the fine motion was 4 μm with a resolution of 6 nm. 
Areal density of ALOT was reduced by a about a factor of two from LAMP to 70 kg/m2.
In 1996 and 1997 , proposals were submitted to NASA for a Next Generation Space 
Telescope (NGST) as a follow on to the HST (see Next Generation Space Telescope, 1997). 
The proposed NGST would be an IR telescope that would be diffraction limited at wave -
lengths of 2 μm and longer. The primary mirror diameter was to be between 6 and 8 m. 
Suppression of instrumental IR background required cryogenic telescope operation 
at <70  K. Because the maximum payload fairing diameter of existing launch vehicles 
(Ariane) was about 4.5 m, a segmented primary mirror, deployed on orbit, would be nec -
essary. Launch vehicle payload capacity limited primary mirror weight to about 1000 kg,

2.9 Scaling Relationships for Lightweight Mirrors
Pearson (1980) defined lightweight monolithic mirrors as those with diameter-to-thickness 
ratios greater than 8. He also suggested that the ratio (surface area)1.5/(mirror volume) > 7 
could be used to define a lightweight mirror. The latter ratio applies equally well to sub -
strates with uniform thickness and those with variable thickness. As an example of the 
use of this criterion, let us consider the series of contoured-back mirrors listed in Table 2.6. 
In each case, the surface area is π(GD/2)2 = π(18.2/2)2 = 254.4 in. 2 Dividing by the mirror 
volumes from the third column of the table, we obtain the Pearson ratios listed in the last 
column of that table. The configurations that meet Pearson’s criterion are the tapered back, 
the meniscus with R2 > R1, both single- and double-arch mirrors. The mirror with a flat 
back, the concentric-meniscus, and the double-concave mirror would not be considered 
lightweight. Parametric analysis of Pearson’s ratio shows that, for constant f-number and 
DG/tA, the ratio is independent of mirror diameter. Further, it varies slowly with f-number.
A direct comparison of open-back, nearly symmetrical sandwich, and single- and double-
arch contoured-back mirrors (see Figure 2.45) was reported by Valente and Vukobratovich 
(1989). Mirror diameter of 40 in. (~1 m) and material type (fused silica) were held constant. 
Self-weight deflection, overall thickness, weight, and efficiency (defined as the ratio of sur-
face deflection to thickness) for each mirror type oriented with gravity parallel to the mir-
ror axis were computed. Certain reasonable assumptions were made by those authors with 
regard to various mirror parameters such as sandwich mirror core solidity, * face-sheet 
thickness, rib thickness, etc.
The results of this parametric analysis were plotted in various ways, so the mirror 
designer could easily visualize the consequences of parameter variations. One figure from 
this study especially pertinent to the present topic is shown in Figure 2.46.
In the same study, fabrication issues and case of mounting were considered. The quali -
tative comparisons made there provide meaningful guidance to the mirror designer and 
user alike. The cost impact of critical decisions can be recognized even though specific 
numerical relationships are not included.

the cell and tW is the web wall thickness.

Cho et al. (1989) presented a comparative analysis of the surface deformations under self-
weight conditions at zenith and horizon orientations for various mirror types (contoured 
back and foam core) with various support types. The results were presented graphically. 
A few general conclusions were drawn: (1) optical performance depends on material char-
acteristics, contoured-back mirror shape, and support location; (2) structural deflection 
over the optical surface does not always indicate optical performance; (3) optimum shape 
of a double-arch metal foam core (SXA material) mirror is a ring support at the 50% radial 
zone; and (4) the optimum support location for three equally spaced support points is 
inside the 50% radial zone regardless of contoured-back shape.
